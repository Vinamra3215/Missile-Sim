\section{Problem}

An agent must fly a missile through a 3D tunnel filled with
static pipes, moving pipes and gates (walls with a single hole),
and strike a target core at the far end, using only onboard range
sensors. This is a simplified version of the navigation problem
faced by drones and other autonomous vehicles, which also have
limited sensing and moving obstacles. So that the agent cannot
simply memorise a route, every episode uses a newly generated
map, and we report results on maps that were never used in
training~\cite{cobbe2019}.

% --------------------------------------------------
\section{Environment}

The simulator is written in PyTorch as a batched environment:
$N$ independent missiles (128 for PPO, 64 for DQN) are stepped
together as tensors, including collision checks and ray casting,
which gives approximately 5k steps/s on one CPU core.

\subsection*{World}

A square tunnel $|y|,|z|\leq 8$ extends along $+x$ for length
$L$, with a target sphere (radius 1.8) at $x=L$ and a random
height/side. Obstacle slots are spaced at least 9 units apart;
each is a pipe (infinite cylinder, random angle in the $yz$-plane,
offset, tilt and radius 0.8--2.6) or a gate (thickness 0.6,
circular hole at a random position). Moving pipes oscillate:
\begin{equation}
  p(t)=p_0+mA\sin(\omega t+\phi).
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Dynamics}

The arcade-flight dynamics use $\Delta t=0.05$ s. Pitch
$\theta$, yaw $\psi$ and speed $v$ are updated by the action,
then the missile moves along its heading $f$:
\begin{align}
  \theta &\leftarrow
  \operatorname{clip}(\theta+1.8\Delta t\,u_\theta,\,\pm1.2),\\
  \psi &\leftarrow \psi+1.8\Delta t\,u_\psi,\\
  v &\leftarrow \operatorname{clip}(v+12\Delta t\,u_v,\,8,22),\\
  p &\leftarrow p+v f(\theta,\psi)\Delta t.
\end{align}

The missile can never stop ($v\geq 8$), so hovering is impossible.

\subsection*{Curriculum}

A difficulty $\ell\in[0,2]$ scales tunnel length
(60$\to$220$\to$400), obstacle count (2$\to$16$\to$30),
pipe size and tilt, fraction and speed of moving pipes
(from $\ell=0.4$), gate frequency (from $\ell=0.3$) and gate
hole radius (4.5$\to$2.0); $\ell\in[1,2]$ is an expert tier.
Maps are sampled at $\ell'\sim U[\ell-0.3,\ell]$ and $\ell$
rises by 0.1 once the success rate over the last approximately
500 episodes near the top level reaches 75\%~\cite{bengio2009}.

% --------------------------------------------------
\section*{Main Contribution}

We present a custom batched 3D RL testbed with procedurally
generated tunnels, static and moving obstacles, collision checks,
and time-paired range sensing. On held-out maps and under a
shared curriculum, we compare PPO and DQN against simple
baselines. The simulator is custom project code developed with
AI assistance, not an existing physics engine.

\section{RL Formulation}

\textbf{State space} $S\subset\mathbb{R}^{61}$ (continuous,
normalised): a $5\times5$ grid of ray-cast distances in a
$70^\circ$ cone ahead of the nose (range 40, scaled to $[0,1]$)
at time $t$ and $t-1$, so that obstacle motion is observable (50);
the unit vector to the target in the missile's body frame (3);
distance to target (1); speed (1); pitch (1); $\sin\psi$,
$\cos\psi$ (2); position in the cross-section $y/8,z/8$ (2);
remaining tunnel length (1).

\textbf{Action space} $A$ (discrete, $|A|=7$): coast, pitch up,
pitch down, yaw left, yaw right, boost, brake
(i.e., $u_\theta,u_\psi,u_v\in\{-1,0,1\}$, one at a time).

\textbf{Reward.} With $d_t$ the distance to the target,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 r_t={}&0.1(d_{t-1}-d_t)-0.01+20\,\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{hit}}\\
      &-10\,\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{crash}}-5\,\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{timeout}}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The dense progress term guides learning, the step penalty
discourages dawdling, and the terminal terms encode the true
objective. A hit is $\|p-p_{\mathrm{tgt}}\|<2.2$; a crash is
contact with a wall, pipe or gate; a timeout occurs after
$\lceil1.8(L+6)/(8\Delta t)\rceil$ steps. Timeouts are treated
as truncations (bootstrapped), crashes and hits as true terminals.

\section{Algorithms and Baselines}

Both agents are implemented directly in PyTorch, without RL
libraries. \textbf{PPO}~\cite{schulman2017} (main): separate actor
and critic MLPs ($2\times256$, ReLU, orthogonal init.), clipped
surrogate objective
\begin{equation}
L=\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\min\left(\rho_t\hat A_t,
\operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,0.8,1.2)\hat A_t\right)\right]
\end{equation}
with GAE~\cite{schulman2016} ($\gamma=0.995$,
$\lambda=0.95$), entropy bonus 0.01, value loss 0.5,
rollouts of $32\times128$ steps, 4 epochs $\times$ 4 mini-batches,
Adam $3\cdot10^{-4}$ with linear annealing, gradient clip 0.5.

\textbf{DQN}~\cite{mnih2015} (baseline): Double DQN~\cite{hasselt2016}
with a dueling head~\cite{wang2016}, 3-step returns, Huber loss,
replay buffer $3\cdot10^5$, batch 256, soft target update
$\tau=0.005$, $\epsilon:1\to0.03$ over the first 20\% of training,
$\gamma=0.99$, Adam $3\cdot10^{-4}$.

\textbf{Baselines:} a uniform random policy, and a hand-coded
steer-to-target rule that turns toward the target and ignores
obstacles (used only for comparison).

\textbf{Protocol.} Each algorithm: 3 seeds
$\times3\cdot10^6$ environment steps, with identical curriculum
(PPO $\approx11$ min, DQN $\approx16$ min per run on a desktop
CPU). Evaluation: greedy policy, 300 held-out maps per level
(separate seed), identical maps for all policies.

Both RL agents do much better than the steer-to-target rule,
which succeeds on 49\% of the easiest maps but almost never once
gates and moving pipes appear, so the agents clearly learned to
avoid obstacles. PPO is better at every level and climbs the
curriculum faster (level 1.2 vs. 0.87 after $3\cdot10^6$ steps),
and the gap widens with difficulty (85.5\% vs. 47.0\% at
$\ell=1$; 55.3\% vs. 8.1\% at $\ell=1.5$). PPO also generalises
into the expert tier beyond the level it trained on (14\% at
$\ell=2$). The three seeds of each method give very similar numbers.

\section{Discussion}

PPO's advantage grows with difficulty. Plausible explanations
(not tested separately here) are its stable on-policy updates
over large batches from many parallel environments, and that
DQN's replay buffer keeps mixing in transitions from earlier,
easier curriculum levels, so its value estimates lag behind the
changing map distribution.

\textbf{Limitations:} simplified arcade dynamics (no aerodynamics
or roll), discrete actions, and a fixed training budget; the expert
tier remains unsolved, suggesting longer training, recurrent
policies or continuous control as future work. A 3D viewer (Ursina)
renders trained policies and supports human play for comparison.
